Captions: Turn Them On, Keep Them On

Captions are the single most under-used kindness in video calling. They help people with hearing differences — and also anyone in a loud kitchen, anyone whose video is choppy, anyone joining from a phone with one earbud in, and anyone who processes a little better when words are on screen too. That's nearly everyone, regularly.

Turning them on says nothing about anyone. It's the video equivalent of turning on a porch light: it just makes things easier to see.

How they work, in one paragraph

Most apps can create captions two ways: built into the app itself, or by someone else's AI helper joining the meeting as a "meeting bot" (chapter 6 covers those, because they also record and summarize, which is a consent question all its own). For everyday use, prefer the captions built into the app. They're usually instant, they run on the same app you already trust, and they don't involve a third party listening in.

Zoom

  • In a meeting: click the CC / Captions button (usually bottom-right on the toolbar). If you don't see it, click More (⋮ or ^) and choose Captions.
  • The host may need to enable captions once — tell them you'd love captions on and they'll usually flip it on from Settings → Meeting → Captions.
  • Zoom also lets you change caption size on the same menu. Bigger is better, always.

Google Meet

  • Click the Turn on captions (CC) button near the bottom right.
  • On a phone, tap the three dots (⋮) → Turn on captions.
  • Meet hides captions when someone is screen sharing — a plain-language note for the room: "the captions nap while someone's sharing their screen" saves confusion.

Microsoft Teams

  • Select More (⋮) → Language and speech → Turn on live captions.
  • Captions can be translated into another language on some plans — a quiet superpower for calls that span a family that speaks two languages.

FaceTime and phones

  • iPhone/iPad: with Live Captions turned on in Settings → Accessibility → Live Captions, captions appear during FaceTime and many other calls. One toggle, system-wide.
  • WhatsApp and Signal keep things simple: no live captions on most phones yet — those calls stay audio-first, which is one reason many families use FaceTime or Meet for the video visits.

Reading captions comfortably

  • Captions lag a second or two behind speech. That's normal, not a malfunction.
  • They're not perfect: a new grandchild's unusual name, a doctor's term, or a Scottish uncle will trip them. Nobody expects captions to be flawless; they're a helper, not a transcript.
  • If a call needs a correct record — a legal, medical, or work meeting, say — that veers into recording territory, which is exactly where the note-taker consent rules in chapter 6 live.

Asking for captions, politely

You never need a reason. A plain request works, always:

  • "Would you mind turning captions on? They help me follow along."
  • "Captions off would be fine for me, but on is a kindness for one of our guests — may I?"

If you're the host and someone asks you: yes, unashamedly. Turning captions on for a call you host is hosting well.
