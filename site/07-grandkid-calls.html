Grandkid Call Setups

Family calls live or die on one thing: whether the little ones find them fun. This chapter is about making video visits work for everyone at once — a grandma who wants faces and stories, parents who want life a little easier, and kids aged 2 to 12 who want something to do.

The two-minute setup that works for almost every family

One stationary device for the grandparents, one moving device for the family. That's the whole design. Grandparents sit; grandkids bounce. Don't fight it — build for it:

  • Grandparents' side: a tablet or laptop propped at eye level (that stack of books again), close enough to be heard, wide enough to show the whole face. Worth aiming the camera so the visiting spot — couch, kitchen table — is in frame before the call starts, so there's no "can you see me?" during story time.
  • Family's side: whatever device the parents already use, on wi-fi at home. The adult holds the framing; kids drift in and out of shot as they please. That's normal and fine.

Casting spells that actually land (ages 2–5)

The magic at this age is short, physical, and repetitive:

  • Show, don't just tell. Hold things up to the camera, slowly, close to the lens: the new slippers, the fish count, the broken cookie. Two seconds of object-peering beats five minutes of "how's school?"
  • The sticker game. "When you see Grandpa, hold up your sticker!" Simple, repeatable, works across three time zones.
  • Sing the same two songs every time. Repetition is the fun. Twinkle Twinkle, then Happy Birthday for anyone, then blow-a-kiss, hang up, done. Eight minutes max — ending while it's still sweet is the pro move.
  • Name the call. "It's Grandma time!" A named ritual with a beginning (a wave) and an end (a kiss, then off) feels safe; a call that goes on past attention span feels like nothing at all.

Ages 6–11: now it becomes a real visit

  • Show and tell with a topic. "Bring one thing you did this week" gives the child the floor and gives the grandparents something specific to ask about. Works virtually every time.
  • Play a game across the wire: twenty questions, I-spy in your room, rock-paper- scissors (grandparents can genuinely lose, which kids adore), or a shared drawing — each of you draws a monster and reveals at the end.
  • Let the child operate the camera. Handing the device over and letting them give Grandpa "the tour" of the bunk bed fort is a franchise hit and gives the parents a two-minute breather.
  • Protect the one-on-one. When age allows (say 8+), a call without parents in the room — grandparent and grandkid, just them — builds its own special channel. Set it up separately from the big family call so the group dynamics don't swallow it.

Reading together over video

Story time translates surprisingly well, with three adjustments:

  • Hold the book at a slight angle to the camera, close, and slow your page-turns — eyes follow the picture, not the plot.
  • Prop the reader's camera at eye level so the child sees the reading face, not the ceiling.
  • Or each side reads from its own copy of the same book and the child turns pages on cue. Library trucks and used-book shops are your friends here.

The grown-ups' side, in three kindnesses

  • Short beats long, always. Two five-minute calls a month beat one half-hour. End before anything sours. A good visit that ends too soon is a good visit.
  • Let the parents set the schedule. No guilt, no "we never see them" — just "what day works? Tuesday 6:30?" Say yes without grumbling, and call on time to the minute.
  • Know the wi-fi reality. If the kids are running through the house mid-call, the picture freezing is normal and nobody's fault. Chapter 8 has the friendly fixes — the one-liner that saves it: "We'll hear you better than we see you; keep talking, you don't have to fix anything."

The recurring-call calendar, made easy

The family dial-in card is exactly for this: one page with the app, the name, the time, and the backup plan, so nobody ever has to text "what link do we use again?" Monday 6:30, Grandma's tablet, FaceTime, Mom's phone as backup — that's all the card says, and it holds the whole ritual together.
