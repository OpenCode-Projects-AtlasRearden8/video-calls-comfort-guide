# Framing and Light: The Free Polish

Two knobs control 90% of how good you look on video: **where the camera sits** and
**where the light comes from**. Both are free. Here's how to turn them.

## The eye-level rule (the single best trick)

Put the camera at **your eye level**. Not below it, not at the ceiling.

- Laptop on a desk? Raise the laptop with a stack of books until the top edge of the
  screen is near eye level.
- Phone on a table? Prop it against a mug on that same stack of books.
- Purpose, in plain words: at eye level, you look like a person talking to a person.
  From below, the up-the-nose angle makes you look like you're dozing off in a soap
  opera. (You're not. Nobody needs that read.)

## Framing: the headroom rule

Sit so that your picture has:

- your **head** in the top half, not scrolled way up with empty wall behind it, and
- a small sliver of room **above** your hair (about a hand's width of air), and
- your shoulders visible. All-forehead tips everyone off that they're mis-framed.

Comfort check: look at your own preview once, adjust the chair or the book stack, done.
We're not staging a headshot. We're making it feel like the two of you are sitting
across a kitchen table.

## The look-around test

Before you hit join, glance at what's *behind* you:

- **Can you see beyond me?** If your back faces a window, the camera thinks the huge
  bright thing is what you want it to look at, and you become a silhouette. Face the
  window and your face lights up instead. Give the window a *little* work to do — let
  it be the light; you take the shine.
- **Lights off, sunset on?** Overhead ceiling lights put raccoon-ish shadows under your
  eyes. Turn on a movable desk lamp or a table lamp and put it roughly in **front or
  to the side** of your face — you'll un-shadow immediately.
- **Room too dark overall?** Same remedy, one step further: a ring light or a desk lamp
  placed at about the 10 o'clock position from where you sit. It's not about the light's
  *brightness*; it's about the *angle*.

## The one-glance tidy (real, but short)

Frame showing a laundry basket or a closed door behind you? That's fine. Truly fine.
If you *would* like a calmer backdrop quickly:

- Pick a wall angle with the least "stuff" in it — lean your chair a quarter-turn and
  face the emptier wall.
- If the app gives you the option, soft blur backgrounds work fine too and are widely
  available. Just be careful on the far side: blurring **your** background says
  "home is home." That's honest and everyone understands.
- Plants, books, and a lamp behind you look warm on camera, same as they do in person.

## The wave test (30 seconds, catches almost everything)

Right before joining:

1. Look at your own preview.
2. **Wave** at the camera — watch your hand. It should land *inside* the frame at
   shoulder height.
3. Lift your chin and smile as if across the table. If your chin disappears from the
   top of the frame, you're too close. Sit back a couple inches.
4. Look at your face: are you lit softly and evenly? If one half is in shade, move the
   desk lamp to the shady side.

That's it. The wave test takes less than half a minute and fixes most "why do I look
wrong" mysteries — the honest answer usually being either the camera sat too low or the
light sat behind you.

## Sitting posture, kindly

- Sit *upright but relaxed*, like you're leaning slightly over coffee rather than
  watching a play from the back row. It reads as *engaged* on camera, not stiff.
- If you use a chair with wheels, lock them. Rolling backward mid-sentence surprises
  everyone, most of all you.
- One nice-to-have: put **a sticky note beside the camera** with the things you want to
  remember to say — or the name of the person calling. Here's why: when people search for
  their words, they tend to look down or off-screen, which reads on the other side as
  them being talked past. Looking *near* the camera for two seconds reads as eye
  contact, because the camera sits about where your eyes would be.
