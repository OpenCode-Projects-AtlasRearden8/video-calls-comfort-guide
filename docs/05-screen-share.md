# Screen Sharing Without Leaking Your Tabs

Screen sharing is where video calls earn their keep: showing photos, a calendar, an
insurance form, the family recipe that absolutely must be corrected. It's also the
single most common place people accidentally show something private — an email with
another person's name, a doctor-portal tab, last week's argument thread.

This chapter is the privacy trick that changes everything:

> **Share the one window, never the whole screen.**

When the share dialog opens, most apps give you a choice: **Screen**, **Window**, or
**Tab**. Choose *Window* or *Tab*, every time. Then only that one window travels; the
other seventeen tabs stay yours.

## Before you share: the 10-second sweep

1. Close or minimize anything you wouldn't taped to the fridge. (Not tidiness — privacy.)
2. Pause any music or video — its sound will ride along, unmixed, at full volume.
3. Close notification pop-ups where you can — on a Mac, the moon-shaped **Focus**;
   on Windows, click the clock and set **Do not disturb**. Those little toasts ("Dentist
   reminder!") love to arrive exactly during a share.
4. Decide what you'll be *sharing from*: open a separate, fresh browser window for the
   photos or document you want to show, and share *only that window*. Nothing else
   appears, no matter what you click.

## How to do it, per app

- **Zoom:** green **Share Screen** button → choose the **window** (photos, PDF, or
  browser tab) rather than "Screen 1". If you use *Share Screen → Whiteboard*, you get
  a blank page instead of showing your desktop at all — great dry run for a nervous sharer.
- **Google Meet:** **Present now** → choose **A tab** (you pick it in the box that
  appears) rather than **Your entire screen**.
- **Microsoft Teams:** the **Share (⬆)** tray → under **Window**, pick the specific one.
- **FaceTime (Mac):** the SharePlay button shares an app or a *window* — same principle.

## The five habits that keep it safe

- **One window, fresh and small.** Open a new browser window with only the thing
  you're showing. Old tab clutter never even gets the chance.
- **Look at the shared thing, not at other things.** The share follows *the window*,
  not your attention — but your cursor and clicks live in whatever you've focused.
  If you must check something else, pause the share first (one click: **Stop Share**).
- **Stop sharing the second you're done.** Saying "pausing here, back in a sec" and
  clicking stop is completely normal and gives you your privacy back mid-meeting.
- **Practice once alone.** Share a window to yourself in a solo meeting (Zoom lets you
  start a meeting with just you). Two minutes of practice and the real calls are easy.
- **When you're the host,** Zoom and Teams let *you* pause or stop anyone's share, and
  to limit sharing to you or to specific people. Family call? Everyone may share.
  Doctors'-office training? Host-only.

## What NOT to share, ever

When in doubt, the things to keep off shared screens are:

- Anything with someone's number, address, medical details, or the content of a
  private conversation — yours or anybody else's.
- Passwords, banking, tax docs, pay stubs, anything "official and about *us* as people."
- Open email inboxes (behind the shared window they may still peek over your shoulder
  if you accidentally share "Screen 1" — one more reason to share a *window*).
- A shared-Photos library in "recent" mode — scrolling too far finds other people's
  other pictures quickly.

## Sharing photos with family, the graceful way

- In a fresh window, open the **exact album** (Photos, Google Photos, or a shared
  album someone made for the trip) instead of "All Photos." Then the "random photo of
  Dad" mystery never happens.
- Stop and let people look. The #1 family-share mistake: zooming through 60 photos
  in 90 seconds. Slide 3, pause ten seconds, tell one sentence of story — "this was
  the lake we got lost at" — then move on. Stories, not slideshows.
